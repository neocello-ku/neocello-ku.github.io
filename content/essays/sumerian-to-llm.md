# Notes from Between Sumerian and LLMs — Crossing Two Corpora

## 1. Forgetting and Awakening — Facing Forward with Eyes That Once Read the Past

It started in the army. That was when I read Edward Said's *Orientalism*. Faced with his claim that the West did not "understand" the East but "invented" it, I began for the first time to wonder whose eyes the Middle East and the ancient world I knew had passed through before they reached me. That doubt sent me upstream. From translations to the original texts, and further back, all the way to Egyptian. I did not want to take things as others had rendered them. I wanted to decipher them with my own eyes. For me, reading the originals had little to do with piling up knowledge. It was a way of pushing back against a kind of violence: a way of seeing that someone else had laid out for me.

**In my twenties I read original texts; in my fifties I read AI, and I build it too.** Yet the person studying AI now is no different from the one who started reading original texts back then.

Following the doubt I had picked up in the army, I studied language science as an undergraduate after my discharge and entered classical philology in earnest. In graduate school I went deeper into ancient languages and comparative religion. I spread old languages out on one desk: Sumerian, Akkadian, Arabic, Hebrew, Sanskrit, Greek, Latin. The Code of Hammurabi in Akkadian cuneiform, the inscriptions of the Achaemenid kings in Old Persian, the Nag Hammadi texts in Sahidic Coptic. I read the originals one character at a time, without going through anyone's translation. What I learned there was less a grammar than a question. **"Why did this text survive, and who decided?"** Behind almost every text that lasted, there was a power that chose it, copied it, and kept it.

This question weighed on me more than most for a personal reason: the sorrow I felt in graduate school reading the Nag Hammadi texts, the Gospel of Thomas, and the Gospel of Mary. Most of the people who wrote them thought of themselves as Christians. But when another side became the mainstream, they became "heretics," and their writings disappeared or were buried in the ground. As I see it, that happened less because they were wrong than because they lost. The line between mainstream and margin is often drawn this way, by the winners, after the fact. Only much later did I notice that the same line is being drawn now in the training data of LLMs.

After that I lived in the IT industry as someone who builds things. I built mobile services, and I founded a platform and grew it. I built multilingual voice AI, helped the auto industry with its AI transformation, and now I build AI for logistics. The field changed several times, but I was always somewhere that dealt with data and language. Along the way I forgot the question I had carried at the old desk.

But as I built, I kept running into that forgotten question. When a client said "add an Arabic voice," we first had to settle "which region, whose Arabic?" When we merged maintenance data from several car brands, the same part had a different name at each company. Picking which name became the standard meant picking whose way of doing things we would follow. Choosing data looks like technical work, but it is really deciding what to keep and what to throw away. That is exactly what scribes and philologists did for a very long time.

Most of the talk in the AI industry these days is about "bigger and faster": bigger models, more tokens, higher benchmark scores. Governments raise the banner of Sovereign AI and jump into a race to build models in their own languages. Along with warnings that web data is running out, the next LLM is sometimes trained on synthetic data that LLMs produced. In this race, some questions rarely get asked. Who chose the text the model learned from? Which languages, whose voices, were left out? If you take a model that leans on English underneath and dress it in Korean, is it a Korean model?

I see this as hegemony, or in plain terms, **a power game over who holds the board**. It used to be about which empire's language became the language of scholarship and administration. Sumerian to Akkadian, Latin to English. Now it is about whose language data, and which company's filtering standards, become the LLM's common sense. The board has changed, but the rules of the game are remarkably similar. The trouble is that today's AI discourse, especially in industry, mostly talks about this game as a performance race and pushes the question of power to the back.

This essay is a notebook that goes back and forth between my old gaze, turned toward the past, and my present one, turned toward the future, revisiting and sorting out old thoughts. *Instead of disappearing in the LLM era, might the philologist's professional ethos become necessary again?* I take up this question in five sections.

How data becomes "canon" (section 2), how meaning gets made (section 3), how language and power interlock (section 4), who gets to portray whom (section 5), and what a philologist has to do in between (section 6).

## 2. Corpus — The Traces Canonization Leaves

**The claim that LLMs learned "the whole internet" is not true.** Someone decided where to scrape and what to filter out. This section asks one question: who chose the "text of the world" that LLMs learned?

In philology, the word corpus cannot be pulled apart from canon. Much of what we now call the Sumerian mythological corpus survived because scribes at the *Edubba'a* (cuneiform school) in the early second millennium BCE copied it out as study texts. The text of the Hebrew Bible that we call the Masoretic Text was fixed by the Masoretes in the 6th–10th centuries. They added vowel signs and accents to a consonantal text that had hardened long before. The Masoretic Text differs from the Septuagint in more than a few places, because the two traditions branched off from different Hebrew manuscripts in the first place. The Septuagint is the Greek text used by the Jews of the Alexandrian diaspora. The Nag Hammadi codices, which we call Coptic Gnostic texts, were buried in the fourth century, around the time orthodox Christianity was settling its canon. One interpretation holds that a nearby monastery buried them after Athanasius banned books outside the canon in 367. Scholars still treat this as a hypothesis, but I learned Sahidic Coptic and felt my way through these books in the original, and there I caught a glimpse of how the burial began. So I hold it as settled. If someone had not buried those books in a jar in the Egyptian desert, many Gnostic texts would have come down to us only as shadows inside the condemnations their opponents left behind.

The Nag Hammadi codices were found in 1945 in a sealed jar near Nag Hammadi in Egypt. There were twelve codices written in Coptic and loose leaves from a thirteenth, and among them was the Gospel of Thomas, a collection of 114 sayings of Jesus. In the Gospel of Mary, from a similar strand (preserved in the Berlin Codex), Mary Magdalene appears as a disciple who passes Jesus' teaching on to the others. Peter objects: "Did the Lord really speak with a woman privately, without our knowing?" Yet in the later memory of the Western church, Mary became the "repentant prostitute." A sermon by Pope Gregory I in 591 fixed that image. The Catholic Church corrected it in its liturgical calendar only in 1969. The stigma had stuck for nearly 1,400 years. Some research argues that the very name "Gnosticism" is closer to a category built later out of the polemics of ancient opponents (Karen King, *What Is Gnosticism?*, 2003). What remains my greatest sorrow is less the content of those texts than the length of the stigma.

Canonization, deciding what counts as an official text, is always an act tangled up with power. Who chooses what as canon comes down to who recognizes whose meaning as worth keeping alive. According to tradition, Imam al-Bukhārī (9th century) included only some 7,000 hadith (about 2,600 without duplicates) out of as many as 600,000 in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī*. That choice had a large influence on what would remain as the orthodox memory of Sunni Islam. The Sanskrit Vedic tradition distinguished *śruti* (what is heard) from *smṛti* (what is remembered), and in doing so set up a hierarchy of how much sacredness each text would carry. When a philologist handles a corpus, the first question is *why did this text survive?* Survival itself is already someone's choice.

LLM corpora (Common Crawl, BookCorpus, The Pile, RefinedWeb, FineWeb, Dolma) are also the products of a kind of canonization. From the moment someone decides which domains to collect, which languages to include, which content to filter, and which licenses make a text acceptable as training data, the corpus is no longer neutral. BookCorpus, built in 2015, is a dataset of about 11,000 free ebooks (about 7,000 without duplicates) from the self-publishing platform Smashwords. Its makers called them books by authors who had not yet been published, but the books had in fact already been self-published, and they were collected in breach of the terms of service, without the consent of the authors or the platform. At the same time, it was a decision about what kind of English prose would become canon. The Pile curated 22 domains, including PubMed, arXiv, GitHub, and StackExchange. In doing so it chose what kinds of knowledge would shape the model's thinking. When FineWeb released a 15-trillion-token English web corpus in 2024, it applied a language filter that kept only English, quality filters, and a blocklist of harmful sites. Those criteria effectively redrew the line around what counts as "good text."

English makes up more than 40% of Common Crawl. When a model trained on such data is called a "multilingual model," that is a statistical majority taking the seat of canon. Korean is under 1% (about 0.8% in the 2026 crawls). This is a problem of missing data, and it is also a problem of how power is distributed. Power gets divided over which languages of thought take part in shaping a new scholarly tool called the LLM.

I sat in the seat that made those decisions myself. When we collected Arabic speech data, deciding which region and which speakers to include, and which sentences to treat as the standard transcript, was our job every time. Back then I thought we were setting quality standards. Looking back, we were making a small canon. (I pick this up again in section 5.)

A philologist asks *why the Masoretic Text differs from the Septuagint*. That same eye becomes necessary again for an LLM engineer who asks *why English Wikipedia has nearly ten times as many articles as Korean Wikipedia*. The two questions work in the same form, more than two thousand years apart.

Synthetic data, popular these days, can be seen with the same eye. Teaching the next LLM with text an LLM wrote resembles, in philological terms, making *a copy of a copy*. As copying repeats, errors and biases tend to pile up and the variety of the original tends to shrink. AI research has shown that this happens when a model is trained again and again only on data it produced itself, and has called it model collapse (Shumailov et al., 2024). Some studies find that mixing in original data and using it carefully can reduce the risk, but someone still has to decide what to keep as the original. Canonization, in other words, now repeats through the model's own hands.

## 3. Etymology and Embedding — Two Ways Meaning Gets Made

**How does an LLM know what a word means?** Philology looks for meaning in the history a word has passed through; an LLM looks for it in the neighboring words used alongside it. The two are alike but differ in a decisive way. Because of that difference, embedding alone has a hard time explaining "why."

Follow the Semitic root *ʾ-l*. Akkadian *ilu* (god), Ugaritic *ʾIlu* (the name of the Canaanite high god), Hebrew *ʾēl* (god) and its plural *ʾĕlōhīm*, Arabic *ʾilāh* (god) and its form with the definite article, *al-ʾilāh > Allāh*. Words from the same root widen or narrow their semantic field with era, society, and religious context. In the Canaanite pantheon, *ʾēl* was both the name of the high god and a common noun meaning "god," and within Hebrew monotheism it became the word for the one God. And the plural *ʾĕlōhīm* arrives at a paradoxical place. Grammatically it is plural, but in meaning it points to a single being. Meaning is not on the surface of a word. Every context the word has passed through over time piles up and becomes its meaning.

The same kind of tracing works in Indo-European. From the reconstructed Proto-Indo-European (PIE) root *deywos* (heavenly, divine) branch Latin *deus*, Greek *Zeus* (theos, often wrongly paired with it, is a "false cognate" from a different root), Sanskrit *deva*, Old English *Tīw* (the source of *Tuesday*), Old Norse *Týr* (the war god), and Old Irish *día*. One root split into many Indo-European languages over thousands of years. The splitting followed rules of sound change (Grimm's Law, for instance, which explains the Germanic consonant shift), and all the while the semantic field kept being rebuilt. Comparative linguistics since the nineteenth century is the discipline that has traced these changes in sound and meaning through time (diachronically).

The Semitic root system is interesting in its own right. Semitic words are mostly built by laying vowel patterns over a root of three consonants (sometimes two or four). From the root *k-t-b* (to write) come *kataba* (he wrote), *kitāb* (book), *kātib* (writer), *maktab* (office), and *maktūb* (what is written). It is a kind of abstracted representation of meaning. The root is the abstract core of meaning, and the vowel pattern is the morphological transformation that turns it into a concrete word. *Seen one way, the Semitic root system is an abstract embedding of meaning that natural language evolved on its own.*

LLM embeddings are a tool that carries out, statistically, the insight that meaning lives in a network of relations. The meaning of the token *ʾēl* is compressed into the distribution of all the contexts it appears in. The reasoning resembles an etymologist looking for a word's meaning in the history of contexts it has passed through. J. R. Firth's 1957 dictum "You shall know a word by the company it keeps" became the foundation of distributional semantics, and that line of work runs from word2vec (2013) → GloVe (2014) → ELMo (2018) → BERT (2018) → the contextual embeddings of the GPT family.

When word2vec gave each word a single static vector, the problem of homonyms and polysemy showed up at once. Is "bank" a riverbank or a bank? One vector had to hold both meanings. The contextual embeddings of ELMo and BERT partly solved this by computing a token's representation over the context of the whole sentence it appears in. The same token got different representations in different contexts. It was a stricter way of acting on the distributional intuition that context decides meaning.

But embedding is not etymology. The difference between the two is fundamental.

Etymology is *diachronic*; embedding is *synchronic*. Etymology asks, through sound correspondences, borrowed scripts, and shifts in semantic fields, why this word came to have this meaning. An ordinary embedding only shows how close the word sits to other words in its present distribution. Some research trains separate embeddings on corpora from different periods to trace shifts in meaning (Hamilton et al., 2016), but that too shows what the change looks like without saying why it happened. So embedding, which reveals correlation, can hardly take the place of etymology, which asks about causes. Still, the two clearly start from the same intuition, *that meaning lives in a network of relations*, and realize it with different tools. Work in *computational phylogenetics* such as Bouckaert et al. (2012) tried to reconstruct the Indo-European family tree with Bayesian statistics. It is one case of statistical and computational tools beginning to answer etymology's questions.

And here we meet the question from section 2 again. The meaning an embedding gives you leans heavily on how the majority in the data uses the word. If the space was learned from data that is nearly half English, a Korean word's position is easily pulled toward its relations with English words. Etymology tried to record every branch a word split into, while embedding tends to tilt meaning toward whichever use is most common. Minority uses, dialect meanings, and old meanings often get buried there. Even in how meaning is made, whoever is the majority largely decides the outcome. That is the subject of the next section.

## 4. Hegemony — Language and Power Always Move Together

**Hegemony is a lead that everyone ends up following naturally, without being forced.** The common languages of history have changed several times. When they did, knowledge, money, and power usually moved with them. Today the common language of LLMs is English.

The lingua franca of Mesopotamia (a common language shared across peoples) changed twice in a big way over about 3,000 years: roughly Sumerian in the third millennium BCE, Akkadian in the second, and Aramaic in the first. Interestingly, Sumerian lived on for more than 1,500 years as a language of scholarship and religion after it died as a spoken language. Sumerian texts were still being copied in cuneiform as late as the Parthian period, around the turn of the era. It is not rare for a dead language to stay on as the language of authority. Latin remained the scholarly language of Western Europe for more than a thousand years after Rome fell, and Classical Chinese held its place as the scholarly language of the Korean peninsula, Japan, and Vietnam for more than a thousand years. The Korean peninsula effectively completed its shift from Classical Chinese to Hangul only in the late twentieth century. Vietnam's shift from Classical Chinese and *chữ Nôm* (a Vietnamese script built on Chinese characters) to the Latin-alphabet *chữ Quốc ngữ* cannot be separated from early-twentieth-century colonial policy. *A shift in linguistic hegemony is never only a linguistic event.*

The fate of Aramaic has stayed with me for a long time. From around the eighth century BCE, Aramaic became the administrative language of the Assyrian and Persian empires and was used from Egypt to near the Indus River. The Bisitun inscription, which Darius I had carved in Old Persian, Elamite, and Akkadian, was also found in an Aramaic copy at Elephantine in Egypt. It is a sign that much of the empire's daily business ran in Aramaic. Jesus, too, is thought to have spoken Aramaic in everyday life. The square Hebrew script, the Syriac script, and the Arabic script all branched off from the Aramaic script. Yet today few people speak an Aramaic language as their mother tongue, and UNESCO classifies several of those languages as endangered. The international common language of one era became marginal, and now it is at risk of disappearing. Mainstream and margin do not keep their places forever. I find that fact comforting at times, and chilling at others.

The Mediterranean story is more familiar. Put very roughly: Koine Greek (the Hellenistic age) → Latin (the late Roman Empire) → Arabic (the Islamic Golden Age, 7th–13th centuries) → English (since the nineteenth century). Each shift was bound up with a move in military, religious, and commercial dominance. As the Abbasid caliphate translated Greek philosophy, science, and medicine into Arabic at the *Bayt al-Ḥikma* (House of Wisdom) in ninth-century Baghdad, the center of gravity of knowledge tilted toward Baghdad. In the 12th–13th centuries, translators working mainly in Toledo rendered those Arabic works into Latin (al-Khwārizmī's algebra and Ibn Sīnā's medicine in the twelfth century, Averroes' commentaries on Aristotle in the early thirteenth), and the center of gravity began to tilt back toward the West. In the 1830s the British East India Company replaced Persian, the common language of administration since the Mughal era, with English and local languages. It is a scene that shows how deeply the replacement of an administrative language is entangled with the structure of colonial rule.

The lingua franca of the LLM era is English. English is "the language with more training data," and it also leaves traces on the model's structure of thought. Wendler et al. (2024) looked inside Llama 2 layer by layer and showed that even when it processes French, German, Russian, or Chinese input, the representations in its middle layers sit close to English words. This means that even when a model handles many languages, the central axis among them may be English. If Korean has the same structure, then even an answer that comes out in Korean may run on a circuit of thought close to English.

This is a technical problem and a political one too. The Korean government puts R&D money into Korean-specialized LLMs under the banner of *Sovereign AI*. If such a model is Korean fine-tuning laid on top of a backbone pretrained in English, we have to ask whether it really thinks in Korean or is English hegemony wearing a Korean surface. The question applies to every Korean model built by laying Korean data over an English backbone.

The lead also shows up as cost. An LLM cuts text into pieces called tokens, and in most models the same sentence splits into more tokens in Korean than in English (Petrov et al., 2023). API pricing, response speed, and how much text fits in at once are all set in tokens. Under this structure, people who write in English do the same work cheaper and faster. Just as an old empire's common language lowered the cost of administration, today's common language lowers the cost of tokens.

For someone building AI for logistics and delivery in Korean, this is not abstract. Even for the same feature, a service for Korean users often starts out more expensive and slower. No grand theory is needed to see it: hegemony shows up on the invoice.

Do multilingual LLMs *liberate* many languages, or do they *deepen* English hegemony? Whenever this question comes up, I think of the translators of Abbasid Baghdad. They may have faced a similar question: was putting Greek philosophy into Arabic a liberation of Greek thought, or its Arabization? The translators of twelfth-century Toledo, putting Arabic medicine into Latin, must also have faced the question of whether they were passing Arab knowledge on to the West or appropriating it. 1,000 years later, we ask the same question in front of the LLM.

## 5. Orientalism — The Politics of Representation in the Digital Age

**Representation is drawing someone else on their behalf.** When an LLM talks about "the Arabs" or "Korea," whose gaze does that picture come from? I put to today's LLMs the question Said put to Western scholarship in 1978.

Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978), the starting point of my studies, argued that Western Oriental Studies did not *understand* the East but *constructed* it. According to Said, when nineteenth-century British and French Orientalists studied the Arab and Islamic world, their research was hard to separate from the gaze of colonial power. To define itself as rational, modern, and progressive, the West needed an irrational, stagnant, exotic Other, and the East was made into that Other. His critique went past moral objection and aimed at the structure of knowledge production itself. In his view the Orientalists, whatever their intentions, were building a framework of knowledge (epistemological tools) that propped up colonial power.

Fierce debate followed the book. In 1982, in the *New York Review of Books*, Bernard Lewis countered that Said had lumped Oriental studies as scholarship together with imperialism. In 2002, with books such as *What Went Wrong?*, he stressed problems within the Islamic world. The two men faced off over whether knowledge and power really cannot be separated. That debate has still not ended.

The same pattern repeats in the LLM era. When Arabic data from MENA (the Middle East and North Africa) is used to fine-tune an English-centered model, whose "Arab" world does that model represent? Arabic is a language with pronounced *diglossia* (two varieties of one language used side by side). *Modern Standard Arabic* (MSA, *fuṣḥā*) is almost no one's mother tongue. It is the formal, literary variety that most Arabic speakers learn at school. What people actually use day to day are regional varieties such as Moroccan *Darija*, Egyptian *Masri*, Gulf *Khaliji*, and Levantine *Shami*. Their mutual intelligibility in sounds, grammar, and vocabulary is low, and their speakers sometimes have trouble understanding one another. Many hold that Moroccan Darija carries strong Berber and French influence and that Egyptian Masri keeps traces of a Coptic substratum. Gulf Khaliji has many loanwords from Persian and from the languages of the Indian subcontinent.

When multilingual LLMs handle "Arabic," they lean heavily toward MSA, because MSA is the variety written down most. But being written down is itself a result of canonization. Who decided which variety was worth writing down is already a political question. Since the modern era, the MSA standard has been shaped by intellectuals and the press in cities like Cairo, Beirut, and Damascus. So an LLM handles *Cairo newspaper Arabic* well but is far clumsier with *Marrakech market Arabic*.

When I worked on multilingual voice AI and handled Arabic speech data, I ran into this problem every day. When a client asked us to "add an Arabic voice," the question of which Arabic was never self-evident. If we went with MSA, no single region was satisfied; if we went with a particular dialect, we lost the markets of the other regions. At the data sourcing stage we had to decide which region and which class of speakers to record, and which text to treat as the standard for transcripts. That decision was political every time.

That experience brought me back to Said's critique, which I had known as abstract theory. This time it came as a practical problem of data curation. Recent work such as Mohamed et al. (2020), *Decolonial AI*, and Birhane et al. (2022), *The Forgotten Margins of AI Ethics*, tries to carry postcolonial perspectives, Said's among them, into AI datasets, model evaluation, and deployment decisions. The same kind of question is needed inside Korea. Whose Korea does a Korean LLM represent? Does training data built mostly on standard Korean represent the upper-middle-class Korea of the capital region while turning the Koreas of other regions and classes into shadows? A Korean AI researcher needs to put this question to themselves, keeping even the possibility of *self-Orientalism* in mind.

The question Said raised in criticizing about 200 years of Oriental studies (*who represents whom, and through whose eyes*) did not go away in the LLM era. If anything, I think it has grown sharper.

Gayatri Spivak (1988) called this epistemic violence: erasing people through the question of whose words count as knowledge, with no need for weapons. The authors of the Nag Hammadi texts, Mary Magdalene, the speakers in the Marrakech market, and Korean speakers outside the standard language stand in similar places in different eras. This violence happens even without malice. When I was choosing Arabic speech data, picking MSA meant erasing the everyday speech of every region, and picking one dialect meant erasing the other regions. Not choosing was not an option. That conflict is what led me to write this essay.

## 6. The Philologist's Eye Is Needed Again

Philology is often said to have declined as a single academic field since the late nineteenth century. As it split into comparative linguistics, historical linguistics, textual criticism, paleography, and bibliography, philology as an integrated discipline came to be treated at times like a museum discipline. After structuralism and post-structuralism in the late twentieth century, the interest of the humanities moved from interpreting texts toward theory and critique, and the philologist's place narrowed further.

But in the LLM era, the philologist's *professional ethos* is being called back. What is a professional ethos? When a philologist faces a text, there is a circuit of thought that runs on its own.

> *In what context was this text made? Who wrote it, and for whom? On what medium, in what alphabet? Through what canonization did it survive? What changes did it go through as it survived? How does it relate to other texts of its time? How have later readers read it? On what structure of power did that reading itself rest?*

There are three main places where this circuit is needed in the work of building AI.

**When choosing data and evaluating models** — Which texts go into training and which variety becomes the standard are canonization decisions. A philologist's suspicion keeps these decisions from hiding behind the name "quality filter." Evaluation is the same. If the same question asked in English and in Korean gets different answers, you need an eye that can tell whether that is a simple error or a trace of English-centered structure.

**When building a domain ontology** — While bringing the auto industry's maintenance and parts data together into a single knowledge graph, I ran into the same facts every day. The same part had a different name at each manufacturer. The same maintenance procedure was written in different terms for each vehicle model, and the same function was classified under different labels in the European and Asian markets. When you annotate a single verse of a Sanskrit Upanishad, you also work out what the word meant in the Vedic period, how it changed in the Upanishadic period, and how later Vedānta commentators reinterpreted it. Ontology work and that annotation work are very much alike; only the use of the result differs. *A domain ontology is a domain's canon system.*

**When designing Korean-language AI** — Suppose a system asks in Korean, finds Korean documents, and answers in Korean, but passes through an English-centered embedding space inside. Is it Korean RAG, or English RAG dressed in Korean? English-centered benchmarks and synthetic data face the same question. Only technical understanding and philological suspicion together can answer it honestly.

There is a professional ethos that carries over between the old desk and the one I sit at now. *The attitude of following the context that made a text all the way to the end, an eye that looks for meaning in a network of relations rather than on the surface, the habit of suspecting the power of canonization, and a sense that never forgets the politics of translation.* This ethos is a working tool in the LLM era, not an antique that belongs in a museum.

Human history has made this choice over and over. Scribes chose which clay tablets to copy, the church chose which gospels to keep, and empires chose the language of administration. Now engineers choose the data to train on. The choosing cannot stop. What I can have is the wariness that comes from knowing I am setting up the board right now, and that someone is being pushed off it. That wariness is what the philologist's professional ethos comes down to.

The person who started reading original texts back then and the person studying AI now are the same. Most of us take the models that giant companies build and use them as they come, without knowing what they learned or what goes on inside. Like my old self, holding nothing but translations. Still, there is a struggle to look inside directly: running models yourself from open weights, fine-tuning small models by hand, and using interpretability research to see whether a model leans on English inside (Wendler et al., 2024, from section 4). To me these are the same struggle as when I read *Orientalism* and went back upstream to the original texts. Understanding for myself, in place of a translation. In the end, the oldest way to push back against an imposed gaze is to read for yourself.

One thing is different. In my twenties I read against a gaze that others had set up. In my fifties I read AI and also build it. I have moved from pushing back against the board to setting it up. So now I put the old question to myself before I put it to anyone else.

The question I learned at the old desk was "Why did this text survive, and who decided?" At the desk I sit at now, it changes to this: *"What did this model learn, and what did it not learn? Who decided?"* The two questions are really one. This essay is a notebook for not forgetting that question.
